What Is the Misery Index? Definition, Formula and What It Misses

Arthur Okun's two-number gauge of economic pain: how it works, how politicians use it, and what a real measure of misery would include.

By the Gross Domestic Suffering team at Elton University · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

The misery index is an economic indicator equal to the unemployment rate plus the inflation rate. Economist Arthur Okun created it in the 1960s to summarize how much economic pain ordinary people feel. It is simple and memorable, but it measures only two macroeconomic numbers and none of the health, violence or despair that make up most human misery.

The misery index formula

Misery index = unemployment rate (%) + inflation rate (%)

If unemployment is 4% and prices are rising 3% a year, the misery index is 7. A higher number means more economic discomfort. Economists typically use the official unemployment rate and the annual change in the consumer price index.1

Where the misery index came from

Arthur Okun, who chaired President Lyndon Johnson's Council of Economic Advisers, devised the measure as an "economic discomfort index."1 It became a political weapon in the 1970s. Jimmy Carter used it against Gerald Ford in 1976, and Ronald Reagan turned it back on Carter in 1980, when double-digit inflation pushed the US index above 20, its highest level since the measure began.

Variants of the misery index

Economists have extended Okun's formula to capture more of the economy. The best known is Hanke's Annual Misery Index, published by Johns Hopkins economist Steve Hanke, which ranks countries by unemployment (counted twice) plus inflation plus bank lending rates, minus growth in real GDP per person.2 Countries with very high inflation, such as Zimbabwe and Venezuela, regularly rank near the top.

What research says about unemployment vs. inflation

The original index weights unemployment and inflation equally. Happiness research says they aren't equal. Using large happiness surveys from Europe and the United States, Rafael Di Tella, Robert MacCulloch and Andrew Oswald found that a one-point rise in unemployment lowers wellbeing about as much as a 1.7-point rise in inflation.3

What the misery index misses

The misery index is a measure of economic pain. It can't see most of what makes people miserable:

  • Preventable death. A child's death from a preventable disease doesn't move it.
  • Illness and despair. Disease, depression, addiction and suicide are invisible to it.
  • Violence and unfreedom. Homicide, war, mass incarceration and modern slavery don't register.
  • Informal economies. In countries where most people work informally, official unemployment can be low even when poverty is severe, so the index can make poor countries look comfortable.
  • Animals. Tens of billions of animals in industrial farming lie entirely outside it.

The misery index is also a national average. It says nothing about who bears the pain.

Nobel laureate Paul Krugman made the same point in September 2020. Jobs were coming back, he wrote in The New York Times, yet "despite job gains we're in the midst of a huge increase in national misery."4 Standard economic numbers can improve while suffering grows.

From the misery index to Gross Domestic Suffering

We think Okun's instinct was right: a nation's success should be judged by the misery its people endure, not only by what they produce. Gross Domestic Suffering takes that idea further by measuring suffering directly, with outcome data from the UN, World Bank, WHO and other public sources.
