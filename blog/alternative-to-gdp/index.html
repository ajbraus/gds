Beyond GDP

The Best Alternative to GDP? 7 Ways to Measure Progress

The Genuine Progress Indicator, the Human Development Index, Gross National Happiness and four more alternatives to GDP, compared side by side.

By the Gross Domestic Suffering team at Elton University · November 19, 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer

No single alternative to GDP has replaced it, but seven serious ones exist. The Genuine Progress Indicator corrects GDP for its costs, the Human Development Index and Social Progress Index measure outcomes directly, and the World Happiness Report and Gross National Happiness ask people how their lives are going. Which one is "best" depends on the question you want answered. We argue the most urgent question is how much preventable suffering remains.

Why the world needs an alternative to GDP

Gross domestic product adds up the market value of everything a country produces. It does that job well. The trouble starts when governments, markets and media treat it as a scorecard for national success.

GDP's own architect warned against that. In his 1934 report to the US Congress, Simon Kuznets wrote that "the welfare of a nation can … scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income."1 In 1968, Robert F. Kennedy put it more bluntly: GDP "measures everything, in short, except that which makes life worthwhile."2

In 2009, a commission led by Joseph Stiglitz, Amartya Sen and Jean-Paul Fitoussi concluded that the world needed to shift "from measuring economic production to measuring people's well-being."3 Since then, the search for an alternative to GDP has moved from academic journals to finance ministries. New Zealand built its 2019 national budget around wellbeing,4 and Scotland, Iceland, New Zealand, Wales and Finland formed a Wellbeing Economy Governments partnership.5

The alternatives fall into three families:

  • Adjusted GDP: start with economic output, then add what it leaves out and subtract what it gets wrong.
  • Outcome dashboards: skip money and measure health, education, safety and rights directly.
  • Subjective wellbeing: ask people how their lives are going.

1. The Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI)

The Genuine Progress Indicator is the most direct alternative to GDP because it speaks GDP's language: dollars. Researchers at the think tank Redefining Progress introduced it in 1995.6 It starts with personal consumption, adjusts for income inequality, adds the value of household and volunteer work, and subtracts costs such as crime, pollution, commuting and resource depletion.

The results are sobering. A 2013 study of 17 countries, home to over half the world's people, found that global GPI per person peaked in 1978 and has since stalled, while GDP per person kept climbing.7 Maryland became the first US state to calculate a GPI and still publishes one.8

Strengths and weaknesses

Strength: it compares directly with GDP. Weakness: pricing pollution, leisure and inequality in dollars requires many contestable assumptions, and few countries publish an official GPI.

2. The Human Development Index (HDI)

The United Nations Development Programme launched the HDI in 1990, building on the work of Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen.9 It combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (income per person, on a log scale so that each extra dollar counts for less).

Strength: simple, global and annual, which makes it the most widely cited alternative to GDP. Weakness: income is one of its three pillars, so it still rewards GDP, and it says nothing about violence, mental health or freedom.

3. The Social Progress Index

The Social Progress Index, first published in 2013, deliberately excludes every economic indicator.10 It scores countries on basic human needs (nutrition, water, shelter, safety), the foundations of wellbeing (health, education, information, environment) and opportunity (rights, freedom, inclusion). Because it leaves money out, it can show how well a country converts its wealth into social outcomes.

4. The World Happiness Report

Since 2012, the World Happiness Report has ranked countries by how people rate their own lives on a 0–10 "ladder," using the Gallup World Poll.11 It then explains the differences with six factors: income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity and corruption. Nordic countries, led by Finland, have dominated recent rankings.

Strength: it asks people directly. Weakness: survey answers vary with culture and expectations, and averages can hide the people at the bottom.

5. Gross National Happiness (Bhutan)

Bhutan's fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, declared in the 1970s that Gross National Happiness mattered more than gross national product. Bhutan's 2008 constitution directs the state to pursue it. The Centre for Bhutan Studies turned the idea into an index of 9 domains and 33 indicators, including psychological wellbeing, time use, community vitality, cultural diversity and ecological resilience.12

Strength: it is the most holistic alternative to GDP ever adopted by a government. Weakness: it is designed for Bhutan and can't be used to compare countries.

6. The Happy Planet Index

The Happy Planet Index, created by the New Economics Foundation in 2006 and now published by the Hot or Cool Institute, asks how efficiently countries produce long, happy lives.13 It multiplies wellbeing by life expectancy, then divides by ecological footprint. Wealthy, high-consumption countries tend to score poorly, and small, low-footprint countries such as Costa Rica often rank near the top.

7. The OECD Better Life Index

Launched in 2011, the OECD Better Life Index scores member countries on 11 dimensions, from housing and income to work-life balance and civic engagement. Visitors can set their own weights.14 It doesn't produce one official ranking. Instead it invites users to decide what matters most to them.

Which alternative to GDP is best?

Each measure answers a different question. The GPI asks whether growth makes us better off. The HDI asks what people can do and become. Happiness measures ask how people feel about their lives.

We built Gross Domestic Suffering to answer a more urgent question: how much preventable suffering remains, and where? The philosopher Karl Popper argued that instead of the greatest happiness for the greatest number, public policy should more modestly pursue "the least amount of avoidable suffering for all."15 Suffering has three practical advantages as a target:

  • Consensus. People disagree about what makes life good. Almost everyone agrees that children dying of preventable disease, violence and despair are bad.
  • Measurability. Deaths, disease, imprisonment and hunger leave hard data. We can count them across nearly every country.
  • Actionability. Each indicator points to a policy lever: clinics, sentencing reform, food security, animal protection law.

GDS combines illness, gender and sexual discrimination, violence, unfreedom, deprivation and animal suffering into one transparent score. See the full framework, or read our companion pieces on the limitations of GDP and the misery index.
