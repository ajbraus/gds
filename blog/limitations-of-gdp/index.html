The Limitations of GDP: 8 Things GDP Doesn't Measure

Unpaid care, inequality, nature, health and suffering: what GDP leaves out, and why it matters for policy.

By the Gross Domestic Suffering team at Elton University · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

GDP measures the market value of what a country produces. Its main limitations are that it ignores unpaid work, counts harm as growth, hides inequality, ignores the depletion of nature, says nothing about health, misses free digital goods, ignores leisure and is blind to suffering.

What GDP measures

Gross domestic product is the total market value of the final goods and services a country produces in a given period.1 Statisticians usually calculate it as consumption plus investment plus government spending plus net exports. It is an excellent gauge of economic activity, which is what Simon Kuznets designed it for in the 1930s. Kuznets himself warned Congress that national welfare "can … scarcely be inferred" from it.2

The limitations of GDP appear when we treat activity as if it were wellbeing. Here are the eight that matter most.

1. GDP ignores unpaid work

Cooking, cleaning, raising children and caring for aging parents are essential work, and GDP counts none of it unless someone is paid. The International Labour Organization estimates that people perform 16.4 billion hours of unpaid care work every day, the equivalent of 2 billion people working full-time. Women perform about three-quarters of it. If it were paid, it would equal roughly 9% of global GDP.3 Hire a nanny and GDP rises. Care for your own child and it doesn't.

2. GDP counts harm as growth

GDP adds up spending without asking why it happened. Cleaning up an oil spill, rebuilding after a hurricane, treating preventable disease, building prisons and commuting in traffic all increase GDP. The economists who created the Genuine Progress Indicator called these "defensive expenditures": money spent to repair damage rather than to improve lives.4

3. GDP hides inequality

GDP per person is an average. A country can grow quickly while most of the gains flow to a small share of households. The Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi commission recommended that governments track the full distribution of income, consumption and wealth, because "average" figures can rise while the typical household stalls.5

4. GDP ignores the depletion of nature

When a country clears a forest, overfishes its waters or drains an aquifer, GDP records the income and ignores the loss of the asset. The World Bank's Changing Wealth of Nations series tracks this natural capital directly and finds that some countries grow GDP by running down their long-term wealth.6

5. GDP says nothing about health

The United States has one of the world's highest GDPs per person and spends more on health care than any other country. Even so, Americans live shorter lives than people in comparable wealthy countries.7 GDP counts the spending, not the outcomes.

6. GDP misses free digital goods

Search engines, maps, email and online encyclopedias cost users nothing, so they barely register in GDP. In large online experiments, Erik Brynjolfsson and colleagues found that the median person would need thousands of dollars a year to give up search engines alone.8 That value is real, and GDP doesn't capture it.

7. GDP ignores leisure and time

If everyone worked an extra ten hours a week, GDP would rise. Whether people would be better off is another question. GDP gives no value to rest, family time, community life or the freedom to choose how we spend our days.

8. GDP is blind to suffering

This is the limitation that matters most to us. GDP can rise during an epidemic of addiction, a wave of suicides or a surge in incarceration. In 2015, economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton showed that death rates among middle-aged white Americans had risen since 1999, driven by drug overdoses, alcohol-related disease and suicide, during years when GDP kept growing.9 They called these "deaths of despair." GDP is also silent on the roughly 80 billion land animals slaughtered for food each year.10

Does this mean GDP is useless?

No. GDP is the right tool for tracking recessions, tax revenue and economic capacity. The problem is using it alone. As the Stiglitz commission put it, "what we measure affects what we do."5 If GDP is the only scoreboard, policies that raise output while increasing suffering look like success.

That's why we're building Gross Domestic Suffering, an index that puts the missing costs back in view: infant mortality, early death, suicide, addiction, violence, incarceration, overwork, discrimination and the mistreatment of animals.
